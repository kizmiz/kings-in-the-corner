# Motion, Haptics and Sound ("Game Feel" / "Juice") in Polished Mobile Card Games

Research date: 2026-10-29. Focus: iOS/Android. The platform docs are the strongest sources here (Apple HIG, Android developer docs, Material 3 token source code). Very few card-game studios publish exact timing numbers. Where numbers come from a recreation, a tutorial or a forum, that is flagged.

## 1. Animation timings and easing curves (deal stagger, flight, flip, settle/bounce)

### Takeaway
No major card-game studio (Blizzard, Second Dinner, LocalThunk, Microsoft) has published exact timing numbers that I could find. The best concrete numbers come from platform motion tokens: Material 3 durations of 50–1000 ms, M3 cubic-bezier easings, M3 Expressive spring constants, and Apple's spring guidance. The most-cited Balatro recreation (Mix and Jam) adds card-specific values: hover scale 1.15, select scale 1.25, 0.15 s scale tweens with Ease.OutBack, and 5°–30° rotation "punches". Feedback should start within about 100 ms of input.

### Cited Findings
**Material 3 duration tokens (exact values, from Flutter's material `motion.dart` source):**
- short1 50 ms, short2 100 ms, short3 150 ms, short4 200 ms; medium1 250 ms, medium2 300 ms, medium3 350 ms, medium4 400 ms; long1 450 ms, long2 500 ms, long3 550 ms, long4 600 ms; extralong1 700 ms, extralong2 800 ms, extralong3 900 ms, extralong4 1000 ms — [Flutter motion.dart source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/flutter/flutter/master/packages/flutter/lib/src/material/motion.dart); [Flutter Durations class](https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/material/Durations-class.html)

**Material 3 easing tokens (cubic-bezier control points, same source):**
- `emphasizedDecelerate` = cubic(0.05, 0.7, 0.1, 1.0). `emphasizedAccelerate` = cubic(0.3, 0.0, 0.8, 0.15). `standard` = cubic(0.2, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0). `standardDecelerate` = cubic(0.0, 0.0, 0.0, 1.0). `standardAccelerate` = cubic(0.3, 0.0, 1.0, 1.0). `legacy` = cubic(0.4, 0.0, 0.2, 1.0). `legacyDecelerate` = cubic(0.0, 0.0, 0.2, 1.0). `legacyAccelerate` = cubic(0.4, 0.0, 1.0, 1.0) — [Flutter motion.dart source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/flutter/flutter/master/packages/flutter/lib/src/material/motion.dart)
- Flutter's `Easing` class has no plain `emphasized` constant. The M3 "emphasized" curve is not a single cubic. Flutter lists emphasizedAccelerate/Decelerate, standard*, legacy* and linear — [Flutter Easing class](https://api.flutter.dev/flutter/material/Easing-class.html)

**Material 3 (Expressive era) spring motion tokens (from androidx Compose Material3 source):**
- *Standard* motion scheme. Spatial (position/size): default damping 0.9 / stiffness 700; fast 0.9 / 1400; slow 0.9 / 300. Effects (color/opacity): damping 1.0 with stiffness 1600 (default), 3800 (fast), 800 (slow) — [androidx StandardMotionTokens.kt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/androidx/androidx/androidx-main/compose/material3/material3/src/commonMain/kotlin/androidx/compose/material3/tokens/StandardMotionTokens.kt)
- *Expressive* motion scheme. Spatial: default damping 0.8 / stiffness 380; fast 0.6 / 800; slow 0.8 / 200. Effects are the same as standard: damping 1.0 with stiffness 1600/3800/800. Effects never bounce (damping 1.0); only spatial motion overshoots — [androidx ExpressiveMotionTokens.kt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/androidx/androidx/androidx-main/compose/material3/material3/src/commonMain/kotlin/androidx/compose/material3/tokens/ExpressiveMotionTokens.kt)

**Apple spring guidance (WWDC23 "Animate with springs"):**
- Springs keep both position and velocity continuous. "A spring can start with any initial velocity, so we get a natural feeling where our animation picks up right where the gesture ends." Retargeted springs keep their velocity, so interruptions "feel smooth and natural." This matters when a dragged card is released and flies to its slot — [WWDC23 Animate with springs](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/)
- There are two parameters, duration (a "perceptual duration") and bounce (−1.0 to 1.0). Quotes: "When you're not sure, use a spring with bounce 0", "A small bounce, like around 15%, doesn't feel very bouncy yet, but the long tail feels a little more brisk", "for larger bounce values like 30%, you do start to feel some noticeable bounciness", and "be cautious about using values higher than around 0.4, since they may feel too exaggerated for a UI element." On when to bounce: "Bounce can also make sense when you want an animation to feel more physical, like if it's going to be used at the end of a gesture." Example given: `.spring(duration: 0.6, bounce: 0.2)` — [WWDC23 Animate with springs](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10158/)
- SwiftUI presets: `.smooth` ("no bounce"), `.snappy` ("small amount of bounce that feels more snappy"), `.bouncy` ("higher amount of bounce"). All can be tuned with `duration` and `extraBounce` — [Apple SwiftUI Animation.snappy docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/animation/snappy)

**Balatro-style card feel (Mix and Jam "Balatro's Game Feel" Unity recreation, MIT licensed. This is a recreation, not LocalThunk's code):**
- `scaleOnHover = 1.15`, `scaleOnSelect = 1.25`, `scaleTransition = 0.15 s`, `scaleEase = Ease.OutBack` (overshoot) — [mixandjam/balatro-feel CardVisual.cs](https://github.com/mixandjam/balatro-feel/blob/main/Assets/Scripts/CardVisual.cs)
- Hover: a rotation punch of `hoverPunchAngle = 5°` over `hoverTransition = 0.15 s` (vibrato 20). Select: a position punch upward of `selectPunchAmount = 20` units over 0.15 s. Swapping cards in hand: a rotation punch of `swapRotationAngle = 30°` over `swapTransition = 0.15 s` — [mixandjam/balatro-feel CardVisual.cs](https://github.com/mixandjam/balatro-feel/blob/main/Assets/Scripts/CardVisual.cs)
- Continuous "alive" motion. The visual follows its logical slot with a lerp (`followSpeed = 30`, i.e. framerate-independent smoothing, not a fixed duration). It rotates in proportion to movement velocity (`rotationAmount = 20`, `rotationSpeed = 20`). Each card has an idle sine/cosine tilt offset by its index (`autoTiltAmount = 30`), damped to 0.2× while hovered. The hovered card tilts toward the finger/cursor (`manualTiltAmount = 20`, `tiltSpeed = 20`). There is a drop shadow (`shadowOffset = 20`). Cards in hand sit on a curve, with Y-offset and rotation set by position in hand — [mixandjam/balatro-feel CardVisual.cs](https://github.com/mixandjam/balatro-feel/blob/main/Assets/Scripts/CardVisual.cs); [80.lv on the Unity recreation](https://80.lv/articles/balatro-s-card-movements-shaders-recreated-in-unity)
- Balatro itself runs on LÖVE (Lua), and its code is not open source. Community recreations reverse-engineer the feel rather than publish official numbers — [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/balatro-s-card-movements-shaders-recreated-in-unity); [Defold forum challenge](https://forum.defold.com/t/community-challenge-recreate-balatro-card-effects-in-defold/76854)

**Tutorial conventions for deal/flip (low authority, not from shipped games):**
- Card-animation tutorials usually use a flip of about 0.5–0.6 s with an in-out ease (for example GSAP `rotationY: 180, duration: 0.6, ease: "power2.inOut"`), a deal stagger of 0.1–0.2 s between cards, and `back.out(1.7)` for pop-ins. One SwiftUI tarot write-up argues that cards should never move in perfect unison and adds small per-card delay and duration jitter, because simultaneous motion "reads as a block sliding." It also suggests slower, more dramatic reveal flips — [vp0 tarot shuffle animation in SwiftUI](https://vp0.com/blogs/tarot-card-shuffle-animation-swiftui); [ComponentOne FlipCard docs](https://helpcentral.componentone.com/nethelp/juicecontrols/topic8701.html)

**Responsiveness threshold:**
- Steve Swink (*Game Feel*) defines "real-time response" as a correction cycle under about 100 ms (reported in a review of the book) — [Liz England review of Game Feel](https://lizengland.com/blog/review-game-feel-by-steve-swink/). The related ~240 ms "full correction cycle" figure comes from Card, Moran & Newell (1983), not Swink — [Uni Bayreuth GxD lecture slides](https://medienwissenschaft.uni-bayreuth.de/wp-content/uploads/GxD-07-Interfaces.pdf)

**Apple motion principles that bear on timing:**
- "Aim for brevity and precision in feedback animations." "In apps, generally avoid adding motion to UI interactions that occur frequently." "Let people cancel motion. As much as possible, don't make people wait for an animation to complete before they can do anything, especially if they have to experience the animation more than once." For games: "maintaining a consistent frame rate of 30 to 60 fps typically results in a smooth, visually appealing experience" — [Apple HIG Motion](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion)

### Inferences
- Suggested starting values, built from the tokens above. These are not values from any shipped game:
  - **Pickup/hover:** scale 1.08–1.15 in 100–150 ms (M3 short2–short3) with an overshoot ease (OutBack) or a spring with bounce ≈0.15–0.25. Lift the shadow (larger offset/blur) at the same time. Start feedback on touch-down so it lands under 100 ms.
  - **Card flight (hand→table, deck→hand):** 250–400 ms (M3 medium1–medium4). Use a decelerating curve (emphasizedDecelerate 0.05,0.7,0.1,1.0) or a spatial spring (standard: damping 0.9 / stiffness 700; expressive: 0.8 / 380). On drag-release, use a spring so the card keeps the finger's velocity.
  - **Deal stagger:** 50–100 ms between cards for multi-card deals. Use 100–200 ms when each card's identity matters. Adding ±10–20% jitter helps it read as a physical deal.
  - **Flip:** 250–400 ms for routine flips. Do it as two halves (scaleX 1→0 with accelerate, swap face, 0→1 with decelerate) or a rotateY with in-out easing. Save longer flips (500–800 ms) for dramatic reveals only.
  - **Settle/bounce:** a small overshoot (bounce ≤0.3, or Expressive fast-spatial damping 0.6) only on arrival at a final slot. Keep opacity/color changes non-bouncy (damping 1.0, as M3 effects tokens do).
- Balatro's "alive" feel seems to come less from long tweens and more from continuous low-amplitude signals: velocity-based rotation, idle tilt, and finger-tracking tilt, plus very short (0.15 s) punches on state changes.

### Gaps
- I found no published frame or millisecond numbers from Blizzard (Hearthstone), Second Dinner (Marvel Snap), LocalThunk (Balatro), Microsoft (Solitaire Collection) or Tinytouchtales (Card Crawl) for deal, flight, flip or settle timing. The GDC talks (Ben Thompson's "The Art of Hearthstone" 2014, Derek Sakamoto's Hearthstone UI talk 2015, Ben Brode's "Designing Marvel Snap", Kent-Erik Hagman's 2025 "Doing It Live: Prototyping in Marvel Snap") have video but no transcripts I could access — [GDC Vault: The Art of Hearthstone](https://gdcvault.com/play/1020615/The-Art-of-Hearthstone-Playing); [mobilegamer.biz on Hagman's GDC talk](https://mobilegamer.biz/second-dinner-reveals-the-secrets-of-marvel-snaps-onboarding-and-card-design/)
- SwiftUI preset default values (duration/bounce for .smooth/.snappy/.bouncy) were not stated on the doc pages I fetched.
- The Balatro `juice_up` internals (actual scale/rotation amplitudes and duration) could not be verified from a legitimate source.

## 2. How Balatro, Hearthstone, Marvel Snap, Solitaire and Card Crawl create tactile juice, and what restraint designers recommend

### Takeaway
The juice in these games comes from physicality: cards tilt and rotate in motion, land with dust or "crunch" impacts, lift with scale and shadow, and wobble or follow the finger. Restraint shows up in the designers' own words ("There is a case of having too much sound in the game"), in Apple's HIG (avoid motion on frequent interactions, let people cancel), and in settings these games ship: Balatro's Game Speed (0.5x–4x), Screen Shake control and Reduced Motion toggle.

### Cited Findings
**Hearthstone (Blizzard):**
- Producer Jason Chayes on the risk of going digital: "you run the risk of losing how fun it is to hold cards in your hand and sort of shuffle through them." The team invested in how cards feel in motion: they tilt and rotate as they move. A summoned card throws small puffs of dust when it lands on the table, and some cards show nicks and scratches to suggest physical objects — [The Escapist: Hearthstone devs talk strategy and physicality](https://www.escapistmagazine.com/blizzards-hearthstone-devs-talk-strategy-and-physicality/)
- Hearthstone's GDC 2014 design talk reported that an early "chain" response mechanic was cut partly because it "was a mess visually" — clarity over complexity — [mein-mmo: Hearthstone: The Path to Megahit](https://mein-mmo.de/en/hearthstone-weg-zum-megahit063,32034/)
- Sound team (Principal Sound Designer Andy Brock): the timing of "when you drag a card out of your hand and drop it into play" is fixed, and sounds are designed around those key beats. The team uses "subjective sound design" ("what's the emotional impact of this card?") and a "more rhythmic and musical approach to sound design in terms of pacing." On restraint: "There is a case of having too much sound in the game." The team must decide "what should be heard when, or even not at all." Voice designer Lauletta says the game "need[s] intelligibility" because of its speed: "making sure people know what's going on, while also making it epic and impactful" — [Hearthstone blog: Meet the sound team behind Hearthstone's harmonic design](https://hearthstone.blizzard.com/en-us/blog/23964694)

**Marvel Snap (Second Dinner):**
- Cards "smash into the board with a satisfying 'crunch.'" Turns are simultaneous. A match lasts at most 6 turns, about 3–4 minutes, against Hearthstone's 75-second maximum turns and roughly 9-round games. The author estimated a Snap turn tops out at 35–40 s (timed by hand), with the purple End Turn button doubling as the timer. Snap "doesn't rely on the cursor arrows that appeared in Hearthstone" — [Game Developer: Designers, don't sleep on Marvel Snap's simultaneous turns](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/designers-don-t-sleep-on-marvel-snap-s-simultaneous-turns) (a 2022 article; timer values may have changed)
- Second Dinner prototyped the core game with physical cards in two days. A physical-first origin is consistent with its tactile digital feel — [Game Developer: Why Second Dinner first prototyped Marvel Snap using physical cards](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/why-second-dinner-first-prototyped-marvel-snap-using-physical-cards)

**Balatro (LocalThunk / Playstack):**
- Third-party analysis credits Balatro's "juicy" feedback (audio, animation, escalating score readouts) as a major part of its addictive loop. This is analysis, not a LocalThunk statement — [Balatro game-mechanics sources page (saschb2b)](https://saschb2b.github.io/game-mechanics/games/balatro/sources)
- LocalThunk set strict art constraints (colour palette, art resolution, standardized UI, strict card sets) — [XboxEra interview](https://xboxera.com/2024/03/09/there-is-a-lot-more-design-to-explore-within-balatro)
- Settings: a Game Speed option (players report 0.5x–4x, controlling animation playback speed) and Screen Shake (under the Game tab) — [Steam community discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2379780/discussions/0/4201364375109001844/); [Switchblade Gaming Balatro settings guide](https://www.switchbladegaming.com/game-settings/balatro-steam-deck-settings/). Update 1.0.1 (beta April 2024, released May 2024) added a Reduced Motion toggle. It stops the background swirl and card jiggle and, per PC Gamer's summary of the notes, removes "much of the 'juice' in the game" — [PC Gamer: Balatro's first big balance patch](https://pcgamer.com/games/card-games/balatros-first-big-balance-patch-fiddles-with-fibonacci-and-makes-a-mod-to-odd-todd); [VGC on the first major update](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/balatros-first-major-update-tweaks-difficulty-adds-new-joker-types)
- Mix and Jam's recreation shows the building blocks: scale pop with overshoot (1.15 hover / 1.25 select, 0.15 s OutBack), rotation punches (5° hover, 30° swap), movement-based rotation, idle wobble, finger-tracking tilt and drop shadow. Numbers are in section 1 — [mixandjam/balatro-feel CardVisual.cs](https://github.com/mixandjam/balatro-feel/blob/main/Assets/Scripts/CardVisual.cs)

**General juice canon and the restraint counter-argument:**
- "Juice it or Lose it" (Martin Jonasson & Petri Purho, 2012) takes a plain Breakout clone and adds colour, tweening/easing on position, rotation and scale, particles, sound and screen shake. The advice is to make the shake's direction and intensity fit whatever causes it — [RPG Playground summary](https://rpgplayground.com/research-making-a-juicy-game/); [GDC Vault listing](https://gdcvault.com/play/1016487/contactUs). Sources disagree on the venue: Nordic Game Indie Night vs GDC Europe.
- Counterpoints: Jonasson later argued that piling on polish may cost immersion, and Folmer Kelly's "Don't Juice It or Lose It" argues the approach has "significant weaknesses" — [RPG Playground summary](https://rpgplayground.com/research-making-a-juicy-game/)
- Apple: "Don't add motion for the sake of adding motion. Gratuitous or excessive animation can distract people and may make them feel disconnected or physically uncomfortable." Also: "when a game displays a succinct animation that's precisely tied to a successful action, players can instantly get the message without being distracted" — [Apple HIG Motion](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion)

### Inferences
- A juice hierarchy shared by these games: (1) always-on, low-amplitude life (tilt, idle wobble, shadow), (2) short punches on state change (pickup, swap, select), (3) big moments rationed to rare, high-value events (Balatro's score escalation, Snap's card "crunch", Hearthstone's legendary entrances). Screen shake and particles belong only in tier 3.
- "Restraint" in practice means juice that scales with significance. Routine actions (dragging within hand, drawing a card) get the least motion and sound. Wins, combos and big plays get the most.
- Ship a Game Speed control and a Reduced Motion toggle from day one. Balatro, the reference "juicy" card game, needed both.

### Gaps
- **Microsoft Solitaire Collection:** I found no developer write-up of its card animation timings, auto-complete animation or win cascade. Its settings and implementation were not researched in depth.
- **Card Crawl (Tinytouchtales):** no game-feel or animation write-up from Arnold Rauers turned up. Available sources cover only its mechanics (the selling mechanic as the "biggest break through") and awards — [World of Playing Cards on Card Crawl](https://wopc.co.uk/blog/card-crawl-ios-mobile-dungeon-crawl-card-game)
- No first-party LocalThunk interview discusses Balatro's animation or juice choices directly.

## 3. Pacing and showing an AI/remote opponent's moves so a human can follow a multi-move turn

### Takeaway
No source publishes millisecond guidance for AI-turn pacing. The shipped patterns are clear, though. Hearthstone keeps a persistent history column (red border for the opponent, blue for you, hover for detail) and shows live opponent cursor/target arrows. Marvel Snap uses a glowing name to signal who reveals first, then reveals cards in sequence. Players complain about both extremes: instant AI moves feel fake, and long, unskippable per-move animations feel tedious.

### Cited Findings
- **Hearthstone history bar:** on the left of the battlefield, showing roughly the last 7–10 actions as tiles, newest at top. "The player's actions are shown with a blue border, while the opponent's are shown with a red border." "Mousing over will show the details about each action." It covers card plays, Hero Power uses and combat — [Hearthstone Wiki: History](https://hearthstone.wiki.gg/wiki/History)
- Players criticized Hearthstone's event log because it "remains painfully limited in the amount of actions it can remember" — [TheGameHaus: Quality of life UI issues in Hearthstone](https://thegamehaus.com/esports/quality-of-life-ui-issues-in-hearthstone-and-how-to-fix-them/2017/05/25/)
- Hearthstone shows opponent "cursor arrows" (live targeting/hover). Marvel Snap chose not to, since its simultaneous turns only reveal the previous round's plays — [Game Developer on Marvel Snap simultaneous turns](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/designers-don-t-sleep-on-marvel-snap-s-simultaneous-turns)
- **Marvel Snap reveal order:** the player currently winning (more locations, then total power as tiebreak) reveals first. A glowing outline on that player's name at the top of the screen shows who will reveal first next turn — [PCGamesN: Who reveals first in Marvel Snap](https://www.pcgamesn.com/marvel-snap/who-reveals-first); [Untapped: How priority works](https://blog.snap.untapped.gg/marvel-snap-wiki-how-priority-works). One guide claims the reverse (the losing player reveals first) and conflicts with the rest — [forgeguides](https://forgeguides.pages.dev/en/guides/priority-explained/)
- **Player and developer complaints about pacing:**
  - An AI that acts the moment the player ends a turn "looked unrealistic and monotonous". The developer proposed a short randomized pause — [Treehouse community thread](https://teamtreehouse.com/community/android-timer-dialog)
  - In a card mode in Elder Scrolls Online, players called slow per-card animations tedious once the outcome was decided. They also said slow animations ate into the turn timer, and that effects not touching the opponent shouldn't block — [ESO forums: Please fix the pacing](https://forums.elderscrollsonline.com/en/discussion/comment/8171712)
  - In Dead Man's Draw, AI-triggered sequences between turns took up to ~2 minutes because of slow animations — [Dead Man's Draw forums](https://forums.deadmansdrawgame.com/468082/long-turn-times-inbetween-turns-1-cause-found-solution-suggested)
- **Accessibility guidance relevant to pacing:** "Minimize use of time-boxed interface elements. Views and controls that auto-dismiss on a timer can be problematic for people who need longer to process information... Prefer dismissing views with an explicit action" — [Apple HIG Accessibility](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility). Game Accessibility Guidelines (basic level) list "include an option to adjust the game speed" — [Game Accessibility Guidelines: Basic](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/basic/)
- Apple: "Let people cancel motion... don't make people wait for an animation to complete before they can do anything" — [Apple HIG Motion](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion)

### Inferences
- Suggested pacing for a multi-move AI turn. This is synthesized, not a published number:
  1. A brief "thinking" beat of about 300–600 ms after the human ends their turn. Randomize it slightly so it doesn't feel instant or robotic.
  2. Each move runs as its own beat: optionally lift or highlight the source card (~150 ms), fly it at the same 250–400 ms as human moves, then hold a highlight on the destination (glow, outline or tint in the opponent's colour) for about 300–500 ms before the next move. That makes about 0.7–1.2 s per move at 1x speed.
  3. Add a persistent one-line caption ("Opponent moved 7♥ onto 8♠") and/or a history tray with opponent-coloured entries, like Hearthstone's red/blue history. Then a missed animation is recoverable.
  4. Tap anywhere to fast-forward the rest of the AI turn. Also offer a game-speed setting (Balatro-style 0.5x–4x), which shrinks the beats proportionally.
  5. Make "whose turn" persistent and unambiguous: a glowing name or avatar (Marvel Snap) and a state change on the end-turn button (Snap's button doubles as the timer). Don't rely on a transient banner alone.
- Motion direction can show ownership: opponent cards enter from and depart toward the opponent's side of the screen. Paired with an opponent accent colour on highlights, this attributes moves at a glance.

### Gaps
- I found no source with tested millisecond values for AI move delays in card games.
- I could not confirm how long Hearthstone shows the enlarged "card played" display when an opponent plays a card. The wiki page doesn't cover it.
- Marvel Snap's current turn-timer length and whether it has a reveal fast-forward could not be confirmed from official sources. Twinfinite has a "Fast Forwarding" explainer that wasn't fetched — [Twinfinite: Fast forwarding in Marvel Snap](https://twinfinite.net/2023/02/what-is-fast-forwarding-in-marvel-snap-explained/)

## 4. Haptics: Apple UIFeedbackGenerator / Core Haptics and Android HapticFeedbackConstants, and which card-game events deserve them

### Takeaway
Both platforms say the same thing: use the system's semantic presets for their documented meanings, keep haptics short and crisp, match the haptic's strength to the importance and frequency of the event, sync tightly with visuals and sound, and make haptics optional. Android says that given a choice between buzzy haptics and none for touch feedback, choose none. In a card game, that means crisp ticks on pickup and drop, success/error notifications on valid and invalid moves, and richer patterns only for wins. No haptics on scrolling, AI moves or continuous drags.

### Cited Findings
**Apple HIG "Playing haptics" (verbatim):**
- "Use system-provided patterns according to their documented meanings... If the documented use case for a pattern doesn't make sense in your app or game, avoid using the pattern to mean something else."
- "Use haptics consistently. It's important to build a clear, causal relationship between each haptic and the action that causes it... If a haptic doesn't reinforce a cause-and-effect relationship, it can be confusing and seem gratuitous."
- "Use haptics to complement other feedback... you generally want to match the intensity and sharpness of a haptic with the intensity and sharpness of the animation it accompanies."
- "Avoid overusing haptics. Sometimes a haptic can feel just right when it happens occasionally, but become tiresome when it plays frequently... Often, the best haptic experience is one that people may not be conscious of, but miss when it's turned off."
- "In most apps, prefer playing short haptics that complement discrete events." Long-running haptics "can dilute the meaning of the feedback". However, long haptics "that accompany a gameplay flow can enhance the experience."
- "Make haptics optional. Let people turn off or mute haptics, and make sure people can still enjoy your app or game without them."
- Standard types. **Notification:** Success ("a task or action has completed"), Warning, Error ("an error has occurred"). **Impact:** Light ("collision between small or lightweight UI objects"), Medium, Heavy, Rigid ("hard or inflexible UI objects"), Soft ("soft or flexible UI objects"). **Selection:** "feedback while the values of a UI element are changing."
- Custom (Core Haptics): "Games often use custom haptics to enhance gameplay." The building blocks are transient events ("brief and compact, often feeling like taps or impulses") and continuous events, each with intensity and sharpness ("soft, rounded, or organic" vs "crisp, precise, or mechanical"). Patterns can be synced with audio — all from [Apple HIG Playing haptics](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/playing-haptics)
- UIKit generators: `UIImpactFeedbackGenerator` ("when a user interface object collides with something or snaps into place"), `UISelectionFeedbackGenerator`, `UINotificationFeedbackGenerator` ("successes, failures, and warnings"), `UICanvasFeedbackGenerator`. `prepare()` "Prepares the generator to trigger feedback" (reduces latency) — [Apple UIFeedbackGenerator docs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/uikit/uifeedbackgenerator)
- Accessibility: "Use haptics in addition to audio cues. If your interface conveys information through audio cues — such as a success chime, error sound, or game feedback — consider pairing that sound with matching haptics for people who can't perceive the audio or have their audio turned off" — [Apple HIG Accessibility](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)

**Android haptics design principles (verbatim):**
- "When it comes to haptic feedback on mobile devices, less is more. Too much vibration can be annoying and even numbing to the hands."
- Categories: Notify, Confirm ("confirm a state change in the device following a user action") and Delight ("haptics synced with animations, sounds, videos, or games").
- "Favor rich and clear haptics over buzzy haptics." "**Important:** Given the choice of buzzy haptics or no haptics for touch feedback, choose no haptics."
- "Haptic effects applied to very frequent events, like scrolling or moving a text handle, should be very subtle... More important events... should be stronger." Also suggested: "gradually increasing the amplitude of a sequence of ticks with dragging, dropping or snapping actions."
- "We strongly recommend co-design of visual, audio, and haptic effects." Out-of-sync haptics "can be a bit unsettling... the user may perceive the haptic actuator to be broken."
- Timing: "A good keyclick haptic feedback signal should last between 10 to 20 milliseconds." The actuator "may continue to ring for another 20 to 50 milliseconds after a 20-millisecond input". Avoid `createOneShot` / `vibrate(long)` for feedback because they "might feel buzzy" — all from [Android Haptics design principles](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/haptics/haptics-principles)
- Implementation: `View.performHapticFeedback(HapticFeedbackConstants.X)`. "Unlike other haptic APIs, using HapticFeedbackConstants with a View doesn't require the VIBRATE permission." Constants "will have fallback behavior if necessary." The method "honors the system setting HAPTIC_FEEDBACK_ENABLED." CONFIRM is "a short and light vibration" and REJECT "might be a stronger feedback to signal failure." Legacy `createOneshot`/`createWaveform` "are often too loud for regular haptic feedback." For predefined VibrationEffects: "If an effect isn't available, consider falling back to nothing rather than a discordant alternative" — [Android Add haptic feedback to events](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/views/haptics/haptic-feedback)

**Android `HapticFeedbackConstants`, with descriptions and API levels (from the reference page):**
- `CONFIRM` (API 30): "confirmation or successful completion of a user interaction". `REJECT` (API 30): "rejection or failure of a user interaction".
- `DRAG_START` (API 34): "The drag target has just been 'picked up'". `GESTURE_THRESHOLD_ACTIVATE` / `GESTURE_THRESHOLD_DEACTIVATE` (API 34): a drag passed or retreated past the threshold at which release will trigger the action.
- `SEGMENT_TICK` (API 34): switching between discrete choices. `SEGMENT_FREQUENT_TICK` (API 34): for dense choices, "expected to be very soft".
- `TOGGLE_ON` / `TOGGLE_OFF` (API 34). `NO_HAPTICS` (API 34). `GESTURE_START` / `GESTURE_END` (API 30).
- `CLOCK_TICK` (API 21). `CONTEXT_CLICK` (API 23). `KEYBOARD_TAP` (API 8). `KEYBOARD_PRESS` / `KEYBOARD_RELEASE` / `VIRTUAL_KEY_RELEASE` / `TEXT_HANDLE_MOVE` (API 27). `VIRTUAL_KEY` (API 5). `LONG_PRESS` (API 3).
- `FLAG_IGNORE_GLOBAL_SETTING` is deprecated as of API 33 — [Android HapticFeedbackConstants reference](https://developer.android.com/reference/android/view/HapticFeedbackConstants)

### Inferences
- Suggested event→haptic map for a card game, following the platform semantics above:
  - **Card pickup / drag start:** iOS `UIImpactFeedbackGenerator(.light)` or `.soft`; Android `DRAG_START` (34+), falling back to `VIRTUAL_KEY` or nothing.
  - **Dragged card over a valid drop target** (snap-highlight appears): iOS `UISelectionFeedbackGenerator.selectionChanged()`; Android `GESTURE_THRESHOLD_ACTIVATE` (34+) or `SEGMENT_TICK`. Fire once per target change, never continuously.
  - **Card lands/snaps into a valid slot:** iOS `.impact(.rigid)` or `.medium` timed to the visual contact frame, not to finger-up; Android `CONFIRM` (30+).
  - **Illegal move / card snaps back:** iOS `.notification(.error)` or `.warning`; Android `REJECT` (30+). Use it once per rejected drop, together with a visual shake or return animation.
  - **Round, hand or game won:** iOS `.notification(.success)` or a short custom Core Haptics pattern synced to the win sound; Android `CONFIRM` or a `VibrationEffect.Composition` of primitives with fallback to nothing.
  - **No haptics:** AI/opponent moves (the user didn't cause them, so there is no cause-and-effect link), each card of a deal or shuffle (too frequent; one light tick at most when the deal finishes), scrolling hand fans, hover-equivalents, and menu navigation beyond system defaults.
- Call `prepare()` on touch-down so the drop haptic plays with minimal latency. Gate all haptics behind an in-app toggle as well as the OS setting.

### Gaps
- I found no first-party documentation of the haptic maps in Balatro mobile, Marvel Snap, Hearthstone mobile or Microsoft Solitaire Collection.
- Apple's exact `prepare()` latency window and the conditions under which haptics are suppressed (for example during audio recording, or when system haptics are off) were not on the pages fetched.

## 5. Sound design: what sounds card games use, mix and volume, default on/off, and respecting the silent switch

### Takeaway
Card-game audio centres on short foley (card slide, snap, place, shuffle) timed to fixed gameplay beats, plus musical stingers for wins. Hearthstone's sound team explicitly manages "too much sound" by deciding what is heard when, or not at all. On iOS, game SFX and music should respect the Ring/Silent switch: Apple names game sound effects and soundtracks as audio people expect silenced. Use the `.ambient` category so players can keep their own music. A large share of mobile players play muted (one 2022 survey says 91%), so every audio cue needs a visual (and optionally haptic) equivalent.

### Cited Findings
- **Apple on the silent switch:** "When a device is in silent mode... they also want to silence nonessential sounds, such as keyboard clicks, sound effects, game soundtracks, and other audible feedback. When a device is in silent mode, it plays only the audio that people explicitly initiate, like media playback, alarms, and audio/video messaging" — [Apple HIG Playing audio](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/playing-audio)
- **Audio session categories:** *Solo ambient* ("a game with a soundtrack"): "Responds to the silence switch. Doesn't mix with other sounds." *Ambient* ("a game that lets people play music from another app during gameplay in place of the game's soundtrack"): "Responds to the silence switch. Mixes with other sounds." *Playback* does not respond to the silence switch. Guidance: "don't make people stop listening to music from another app if you don't need to" — [Apple HIG Playing audio](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/playing-audio)
- **Volume:** "People expect their volume settings to affect all sound in the system — including music and in-app sound effects." "Adjust levels automatically when necessary — don't adjust the overall volume. Your app can adjust relative, independent volume levels to achieve a great mix of audio, but the system volume always governs the final output" — [Apple HIG Playing audio](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/playing-audio)
- **Interruptions:** "a game doesn't need to check the interruption type before automatically resuming playback, because a game plays audio without an explicit user choice" — [Apple HIG Playing audio](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/playing-audio)
- **Hearthstone's approach:** sounds are built around fixed gameplay beats such as dragging a card out of the hand and dropping it into play. The pacing is "rhythmic and musical". "There is a case of having too much sound in the game", so the team decides "what should be heard when, or even not at all." Voice lines must keep "intelligibility" because the game moves fast. Layering example: an alien screech processed with EQ and tremolo plus "a layer of pitched hissing... to give it some weight" — [Hearthstone blog: Meet the sound team](https://hearthstone.blizzard.com/en-us/blog/23964694)
- Marvel Snap's card landing is described as a "satisfying 'crunch'": the impact sound and visual land together — [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/designers-don-t-sleep-on-marvel-snap-s-simultaneous-turns)
- **How many play muted (sources conflict):**
  - TapResearch (May 2022, n=2,400 census-balanced, a panel "entirely made up of mobile app gamers"): "only 8% of gamers we surveyed said they play with game sound on." Over 91% play with sound off. Simulation-game players were more likely to have sound on; puzzle and casual players less likely — [TapResearch blog](https://blog.tapresearch.com/how-sound-preferences-impact-player-engagement)
  - Contradicted by 2013 in-app analytics from Amplify/Appington: 73% of players had volume at 10% or higher (58% UK, 78% US, 90% China/Korea) — [TouchArcade 2013](https://toucharcade.com/2013/11/05/73-of-mobile-gamers-play-with-the-sound-on/)
  - A single-game data point: in Dynamite Jack, only about 2% of users turned audio off in settings — [Phil Hassey's Dynamite Jack settings post-mortem](https://www.philhassey.com/blog/?p=1202)
  - The gap is likely methodological (self-report vs measured volume vs in-game toggle) and a decade apart in time.
- **Accessibility:** "Augment audio cues with visual cues." Pair success chimes or error sounds "with matching haptics for people who can't perceive the audio or have their audio turned off" — [Apple HIG Accessibility](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)

### Inferences
- A card-game sound palette: soft slide or whoosh on drag/flight, a crisp snap or tap on landing (louder for valid placement, a duller "thunk" or reversed swish for snap-back), a riffle or bridge for shuffle (one sound per shuffle, not per card), a quiet tick per dealt card with pitch or volume variation (or a single deal sound), a rising chime or stinger for scoring or combos, and a short musical flourish for wins.
- To avoid noise: randomize pitch (±3–5%) and sample choice on repeated SFX. Cap simultaneous instances (voice-limit deal ticks). Duck music briefly under important stingers. Never play sounds for actions the user can't see. For AI turns, use the same placement sounds at slightly lower gain so the human's own actions stay dominant.
- Defaults: on iOS, use `AVAudioSession.Category.ambient`. That respects the silent switch (so "on by default" is safe), mixes with the user's music, and needs no custom detection. Provide separate in-app Music and SFX sliders or toggles. Because many players are muted, the game must be fully legible without sound.

### Gaps
- I found no published loudness targets (LUFS/dBFS) or SFX-vs-music mix ratios for mobile card games.
- Nothing documents an industry "default on/off" convention for game SFX or music on mobile. The Apple guidance implies on by default, governed by the silent switch and system volume.
- I didn't find an Android equivalent of the iOS silent switch rule. Android's ringer/DND modes and media-volume behaviour for games were not researched.

## 6. Reduced motion: honoring iOS Reduce Motion / Android Remove animations / prefers-reduced-motion without losing clarity

### Takeaway
Apple's guidance is specific: when Reduce Motion is on, cut automatic and repetitive animation (zooming, scaling, peripheral motion), tighten springs to remove bounce, replace x/y/z travel with fades, and avoid animated depth and blur. Never make motion the only carrier of information. Balatro's 1.0.1 Reduced Motion toggle (removing background swirl and card jiggle) and the Xbox and Game Accessibility Guidelines (options to disable camera/screen shake and background motion, a game-speed option) show how this works in games. On Android, the "Remove animations" setting sets the animator duration scale to 0.

### Cited Findings
- **Apple HIG Accessibility (verbatim):** "Be cautious with fast-moving and blinking animations... People who are prone to these effects can turn on the Reduce Motion accessibility setting. When this setting is active, ensure your app or game responds by reducing automatic and repetitive animations, including zooming, scaling, and peripheral motion. Other best practices for reducing motion include: Tightening animation springs to reduce bounce effects; Tracking animations directly with people's gestures; Avoiding animating depth changes in z-axis layers; Replacing transitions in x-, y-, and z-axes with fades to avoid motion; Avoiding animating into and out of blurs" — [Apple HIG Accessibility](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)
- **Apple HIG Motion:** "Make motion optional. Not everyone can or wants to experience the motion in your app or game... avoid using it as the only way to communicate important information" — [Apple HIG Motion](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion)
- **Balatro:** the Reduced Motion toggle (1.0.1, 2024) stops cards jiggling and the background swirling, removing "much of the 'juice'." Game Speed and Screen Shake are separate controls. Some players with motion sickness had earlier complained that the background still moves and cards change size, which predates the toggle — [PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/games/card-games/balatros-first-big-balance-patch-fiddles-with-fibonacci-and-makes-a-mod-to-odd-todd); [Steam community discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2379780/discussions/0/4346606879521037914)
- **Xbox Accessibility Guideline 117 (verbatim):** "Avoid the use of camera shake, camera bobbing effects, motion blur... or provide an option to turn off these behaviors." When moving, blinking or flashing content shares a screen with text, provide "A mechanism to entirely disable this content" and "A mechanism to pause or hide this content." "Avoid any repetitive side-to-side or up-and-down on-screen movement, except that which is core to game play." Example: Halo Infinite lets players set screen shake and full-screen effects "across a sliding scale from zero to 100%" — [Xbox Accessibility Guideline 117](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/117)
- **Game Accessibility Guidelines:** "Provide an option to turn off / hide background movement" and "Include an option to adjust the game speed" (basic level); "avoid flickering images and repetitive patterns" (intermediate) — [Game Accessibility Guidelines: Basic](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/basic/); [Intermediate](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/intermediate/)
- **Android:** the Accessibility → "Remove animations" toggle sets the animator duration scale to 0, so Animator-based animations end immediately. Apps can read `Settings.Global.ANIMATOR_DURATION_SCALE` (treat 0 as reduced motion, default 1f) — [DEV: Android animations and reduced motion (Eevis Panula)](https://dev.to/eevajonnapanula/android-animations-and-reduced-motion-2em2). Caveat: a React Native issue reports that "Remove animations" didn't make `AccessibilityInfo.isReduceMotionEnabled()` return true at the time — [react-native issue #31221 (via Lightrun)](https://lightrun.com/answers/facebook-react-native-android-remove-animations-setting-isnt-reflected-when-using-accessibilityinfo). Note that a game engine's own tweens (Unity, Godot, custom loops) will not automatically obey the system animator scale. The game must read the setting itself.

### Inferences
- A reduced-motion recipe for a card game that keeps clarity:
  - **Keep** the information carried by position changes: where the card went and who moved it. Shorten travel to about 100–150 ms with no overshoot, or crossfade the card out at its source and in at its destination with a 150–250 ms highlight glow or outline on arrival.
  - **Remove** idle wobble and tilt, finger-tracking 3D tilt, swirling or animated backgrounds, screen shake, particle bursts, z-depth "lift" zooms and blur transitions.
  - **Replace** flips with an instant face swap or a crossfade. Replace scale-pop on pickup with a static outline, colour or shadow change. Replace springs with zero-bounce (critically damped) curves, like the M3 effects tokens (damping 1.0).
  - **Preserve** turn and ownership signals through non-motion channels: persistent colour-coded highlights, the text caption or history log, and haptics and sound (which Apple suggests as complements).
- Expose an in-game "Reduced motion" toggle that defaults from the OS setting (iOS `UIAccessibility.isReduceMotionEnabled`; Android animator scale = 0) but can be overridden. Keep separate Screen Shake and Game Speed controls, as Balatro does.

### Gaps
- I did not fetch the MDN/W3C pages for the CSS `prefers-reduced-motion` media feature or the WCAG 2.3.3 "Animation from Interactions" criterion. They are relevant if the game is web or hybrid, but are not cited here.
- I found no official Android developer page (as opposed to blogs) that sets out reduced-motion design guidance comparable to Apple's list.
- Apple's iOS sub-options ("Prefer Cross-Fade Transitions", "Auto-Play Animated Images") and their API flags were not verified this session.
